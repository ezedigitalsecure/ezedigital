Why aren't kids excited about cyber security? (And how we can change that)

By Nicki King, Founder, Eze Digital

At a recent cyber security meetup here in Wiltshire, I got talking to someone who works in education. What they told me has stayed with me ever since.

Schools want young people to get into coding and cyber security. The jobs are there, the pay is good, and the country needs them. But a lot of students just aren't interested.

It's not the kids

Young people spend half their lives online. They're curious, quick, and often better with tech than the adults around them. So why the shrug when cyber security comes up?

Part of the answer is who's in front of them. The people who find this work exciting are mostly working in the industry, and teaching salaries can't compete. So computing is often taught by teachers who trained in other subjects and have kindly stepped up. They do their best, but it's hard to inspire anyone about a world you've never worked in.

The highlight: kids don't need more worksheets. They need to meet real people who do this job and love it.

Why this matters to me

Before cyber security, I spent years working with young people. I taught children to swim for 15 years, trained and managed hundreds of teenage lifeguards, and worked in a private school as a nutrition educator, teaching children to really cook: chopping onions, peeling carrots, even butchering a chicken.

What I learned is simple. When young people get hands-on with something real, led by someone who genuinely loves it, they light up. Children who "didn't like vegetables" tried new foods because they'd made them. Teenagers whose parents signed them up for lifeguard training ended up working for me for years, and many are still friends today.

One little boy was petrified of the water when he first came to me. Fifteen years on, he and his mum still send me updates. He's now a helicopter pilot in the US Navy, and he's kind enough to say I played a part in getting him there.

That's what I want to bring to cyber security: real skills, real confidence, and someone in front of them who believes they can do it.

You don't need a degree to get started

One myth I hear a lot is that cyber security is only for maths geniuses with a degree. It isn't. Many people get their foot in the door with a short course and one recognised entry-level certificate, and go on to a well-paid, genuinely interesting career.

That matters for young people, and for anyone looking for a fresh start. They just need someone to show them the door is open.

A passion project: what I'm building

This one is personal. Inspiring young people is what I've always loved doing, and I'm now actively building a programme to bring that same energy to cyber security, for both sides of the classroom:

  • Teacher INSET days that give teachers who've stepped into computing from other subjects the confidence, and the fun, to really enjoy teaching it
  • Workshop days in schools, hands-on sessions that get students spotting scams, thinking like a "good hacker" and solving real challenges
  • Speaking events for students, staff and parents, showing what a career in cyber actually looks like and how to get there

Too often the IT lab ends up as an extra job someone has kindly agreed to take on. I want to change that, bringing a real joy of learning to teachers and students alike.

Longer term, I'm working towards a curriculum and an app to engage young minds and open their eyes to the very real opportunities in this industry.

Our CPD-accredited courses are written for adults and business teams, so they're not right for a classroom as they are. But the heart of our Cybersecurity Awareness for Everyone course, spotting scams, strong passwords and protecting your accounts, is exactly what young people need too. That's where the schools programme starts.
